Convection Between Reservoirs

A hot plate below a cold plate, in one fluid. Warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks, and the two meet in plumes, rolls and swirls. This report explains the physics behind it, how the computer solves it 60 times a second on the graphics card, and how its accuracy was checked.

1What you are looking at

The picture is a vertical slice through a fluid, like air or water. Color shows temperature only: blue is colder than the surroundings, orange is warmer, and near-black is the surrounding (ambient) temperature. It is all one fluid; the plates are heat sources and sinks, not emitters of different fluids.

Everything is measured in domain units: the domain is 1 unit tall and 1.5 wide, and temperature is 0 at ambient, +1 at the hot plate and −1 at the cold plate (both adjustable). Time is in simulation units; the default step is 0.02 (simulation speed 0.25×), and slow combinations such as the very viscous ones use a larger step.

2The physics

Three effects shape the flow, plus one rule.

Buoyancy

Warm fluid is lighter and is pushed up; cool fluid is heavier and sinks. The push is proportional to how far the temperature is from ambient.

Viscosity

Internal friction. It smooths out the motion and damps small swirls.

Heat diffusion

Heat spreads from warm to cool even in still fluid, softening temperature edges.

Incompressibility

The rule: fluid can't pile up or thin out. Whatever flows into a region must flow out.

Written out, these are the 2D Navier–Stokes equations with the Boussinesq approximation, which treats density as constant except in the buoyancy term:

∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −∇p + ν ∇²u + B·T·ŷ ∇·u = 0 ∂T/∂t + (u·∇)T = κ ∇²T

Here u is velocity, p pressure, T temperature, ν viscosity, κ heat diffusion and B buoyancy strength. These are the four sliders under the picture.

Why this setup convects

Hot below and cold above is unstable: heavy fluid sits on top of light fluid. If the push from buoyancy beats the damping from viscosity and heat diffusion, the fluid overturns. Their ratio is the Rayleigh number:

Ra = B·ΔT·L³ / (ν·κ) = 1.7 × 2 × 0.79³ / (0.00035 × 0.0003) ≈ 1.6 × 10⁷

With the default sliders, ΔT = 2 is the difference between the plates and L = 0.79 is the gap between their faces. Between two horizontal plates, convection starts at about Ra ≈ 1,700, so this flow is far past the onset, in the range where real convection is turbulent with thin plumes and large rolls. The Prandtl number ν/κ ≈ 1.2 sits between air (0.7) and water (7), so momentum and heat spread at similar rates.

What to watch for, all of which real convection shows:

  • Plumes with mushroom caps peeling off the plates, where the thin heated or cooled layer on each plate becomes unstable.
  • A large circulating roll that fills the space between the plates and picks a direction (clockwise or counterclockwise) by chance. Different runs lean different ways.
  • Gravity currents: cold fluid spilling off the cold plate's ends and sliding down, and warm fluid climbing past the hot plate's ends.

The controls

The Fluid controls work in two modes. With Dimensionless: OFF you set the fluid's properties (buoyancy, viscosity, thermal diffusion). With Dimensionless: ON you set Ra and Pr directly, and viscosity and thermal diffusion follow from them (section 3 explains why that is enough). The Pr slider can snap to real substances, and always names the substance it matches or the two it falls between.

Each control has a reasonable range, where the simulation can be trusted, and a wider extreme range for seeing what happens beyond it; the green Reasonable min/max button switches to red Extreme min/max. Some combinations leave the reasonable region even inside the reasonable ranges, so a chip on the flow numbers says when the current settings are outside it, and why.

Below the controls, the Flow numbers panel shows Ra, Pr, the measured Re and Ri. Each tile gives the value, what it predicts, a one-sentence plain definition, and the ratio drawn as a fraction (section 3 explains them all), and a chip at the top says whether the current settings are within the reasonable range.

Surprise me applies a random combination from a list of known interesting ones (turbulent air, water plumes, steady rolls, liquid metal, olive oil, crosswind, strong wind, narrow and wide plates, a hot floor, and two found by playing with the page: "Seemingly balanced?" and "Diagonal divide"), All defaults puts every control back, and Copy settings copies the current combination as one line of text, which is how new combinations get added to the list.

3The numbers that describe a fluid

Why setting Ra and Pr is enough: once they are fixed, changing buoyancy or the plate temperatures only changes the flow's speed scale, the free-fall velocity √(gβΔT·L), so the same flow plays faster or slower. In Dimensionless mode the simulation keeps Ra and Pr fixed and works out ν and κ from them. At low Ra that would need diffusion too strong to solve in one time step, so it lowers the buoyancy instead: the same flow, only slower, which is what a low Rayleigh number means in a box this size.

4One time step

The simulation splits the equations into simple pieces and applies them one after another, 60 times a second. This approach, operator splitting with a pressure projection, follows Jos Stam's "Stable Fluids" method, which is the standard choice for interactive fluids.

  1. Apply the edgesSet the plates and the domain edges to their rules (section 6).
  2. BuoyancyAdd B·T·dt to the vertical velocity of every fluid cell.
  3. ViscositySmooth the velocity by solving the implicit diffusion equation.
  4. Make it incompressibleRemove any part of the flow that piles up or thins out (section 5).
  5. Move the velocity along itselfAdvection: the flow carries its own momentum.
  6. Make it incompressible againAdvection breaks the rule slightly, so it is restored.
  7. Heat diffusionSpread temperature, with the plates held at their set values.
  8. Move the heatAdvect temperature with the corrected velocity.
  9. Apply the edges again, then the extrasEdges and plates, then the tracer particles if they are on.

Two grids

Velocity and pressure live on a 288 × 192 grid. Temperature lives on a grid twice as fine, 576 × 384, because temperature is what you see, while the expensive pressure solve stays on the coarser grid. Advection samples the velocity smoothly at the finer cells, and buoyancy uses each velocity cell's average of its four temperature cells.

Advection: looking backwards

To find what arrives at a cell, the solver follows the flow backwards one step and reads the value it finds there, blending the four nearest cells. This "semi-Lagrangian" method stays stable at any speed, but each blend blurs a little.

A MacCormack correction fixes most of the blur: it runs the step forwards, then backwards, measures how far it failed to get back to the start, and adds half that error back. The result is clamped to the values it came from, so the correction can never create new hot or cold extremes. This is what keeps plumes thin enough to roll up into mushroom caps.

Diffusion: red-black over-relaxation

Diffusion is solved implicitly, which stays stable at any step size:

(1 − α∇²) x_new = x_old, α = diffusivity · dt / h²

The solver colors the cells like a checkerboard and updates the red cells, then the black ones, so each update already uses its neighbors' new values (Gauss–Seidel). Each update also overshoots slightly toward the answer (over-relaxation, by a factor of 1.2 to 1.8 depending on α). It runs at least 8 sweeps, and more when diffusion is strong: max(8, 5·√α). Plate cells are skipped, so the fluid next to a plate always sees the plate's set temperature, and zero velocity.

5Keeping the fluid incompressible

Every step leaves some places where fluid would pile up or thin out (nonzero divergence). The fix is a pressure field: high pressure pushes fluid away from where it piles up. Finding that pressure means solving a Poisson equation, then subtracting the pressure's slope from the velocity:

∇²p = ∇·u u ← u − ∇p

This is by far the most expensive part, and how it is solved shapes the whole picture.

Why multigrid

The simple method, Jacobi iteration, sets each cell's pressure to the average of its neighbors plus a correction. Each pass spreads information only one cell, so a pressure change that must reach across 288 cells needs thousands of passes. With an affordable 20–40 passes, the flow stayed in stiff vertical columns, because the domain-wide circulation never got established.

Multigrid solves on a stack of smaller and smaller copies of the grid. An error that is 100 cells wide on the full grid is only a few cells wide on a small grid, where Jacobi fixes it quickly. Each V-cycle smooths a little, hands the leftover error down a level, solves the tiny grid, and adds the corrections back up:

Two V-cycles per projection cost less than the old 40 Jacobi passes and match a 300-pass Jacobi reference. That is what lets large rolls, floor-hugging currents and plumes that curl over appear.

Details that turned out to matter

  • Plates are solid in the solve. Pressure has no slope across a plate face, so nothing pushes fluid into a plate. Before this, 89% of the leftover divergence sat next to the plates.
  • Coarse plates must stay symmetric. On small grids the plate edges fall in the middle of cells. A coarse cell now counts as solid only if it lies entirely inside the plate. An earlier rule made the coarse hot plate thinner than the cold one, which tilted every pressure solve and made the whole flow run cold.
  • The smallest grid needs 40 passes. The 9 × 6 grid carries the single domain-wide pressure mode, which decides how much fluid enters or leaves through the edges. Cutting it to 8 passes looked harmless in a one-step test, but over hundreds of steps cold fluid flooded the domain.
  • Edge strips are not dropped. A coarse edge cell covers a strip of real fine cells, so their leftover error is folded into the neighboring interior cell instead of being thrown away.

6Edges and plates

All four edges are open to ambient surroundings and treated the same way:

  • fluid can flow in or out anywhere;
  • fluid flowing in arrives at ambient temperature, and fluid flowing out keeps its own;
  • pressure is zero at the edges.

With a free stream, the left edge becomes a fixed inlet blowing ambient fluid to the right, and the plates leave wakes downstream.

The plates are solid, no-slip and held at fixed temperature. Their temperature flickers by ±3%, varying along the plate and from step to step, with a new random pattern every reset. Real surfaces are never perfectly uniform, and these small differences are what break the thin boundary layers into separate plumes. Without them a perfectly symmetric start stays perfectly symmetric.

The original sketch had a floor held at ambient temperature, a top that only let fluid out, and sides held at ambient. That made the top run cold and the bottom neutral, because cold fluid near the top had nowhere to go while the floor pulled the bottom back to ambient. Those edges are still available in the code as boundaries: 'original'.

7Running on the GPU

The original sketch ran on the CPU in JavaScript at 72 × 48. The professor's feedback pointed to shaders as the way to much higher resolution, and that is the structure now:

  • Each field (velocity, temperature, pressure) is a float texture, one texel per grid cell.
  • Each step of the method is a small fragment shader that runs once for every cell at the same time, reading one texture and writing another.
  • A GPU can't read and write the same texture in one pass, so each field has a read copy and a write copy that swap after every pass (ping-pong).
MeasureValue
Cells (velocity / temperature)55,296 / 221,184
Shader passes per stepabout 370
Fixed cost per passabout 13 µs
Step time, Intel laptop GPU (power-saver)3.5–4 ms
Frame rate, everything onabout 55–60 fps

Most of the cost is the number of passes, not the data each moves: cutting texture channels from four to the one or two each field needs saved only about 5%. That is why the pressure and diffusion solvers were chosen for converging in few passes.

8Visuals and interaction

Color

The display shader samples the fine temperature grid smoothly and maps it through five colors you can pick yourself: max cold, cold, middle (ambient), hot and max hot. Each half of the bar blends linearly through its three colors, and Average mids sets cold and hot halfway between their neighbors. The color bar in the side panel is drawn with the same stops, so it always matches the picture.

Flow tracers

2,048 particles ride the flow. Each lives in one texel of a texture holding its position, age and lifetime, and a shader moves it with a midpoint (RK2) step. Every step draws a short line from where each particle was to where it is, into a streak layer that fades over about half a time unit. A particle respawns at a random spot when it gets old, leaves the domain or hits a plate; with a free stream, some are born at the inlet so incoming flow is traced too.

The brush

Dragging in the fluid paints warm fluid, and the Mouse button (or M) switches to cool; the button takes the color it paints. The brush eases the fluid under a soft round patch toward the hot or cold plate temperature instead of adding heat, so going over the same spot can never overshoot.

Layout

From top to bottom, the page has:

  • a bar with the link to this report;
  • the simulation, with a side panel for the free stream, the plate temperatures and the fluid temperature colors;
  • the controls in two columns. On the left, Fluid (with the Dimensionless and Reasonable/Extreme min/max buttons) and, under it, Meta controls (Pause, Restart, Mouse, Labels, Tracers, simulation speed and the frame rate). On the right, Setup (plate width, free stream) and Combinations. Each slider shows its value and a one-line hint;
  • the Flow numbers panel.

The simulation keeps its 3:2 shape and resizes with the window; the side panel sits beside it on wide screens and below it on phones, and the control blocks and number tiles rearrange to fit. The color picker is part of the page, rather than the browser's own, so it always opens on screen. Your colors are remembered in your browser.

9How we know it is right

The mirror test

The physics is exactly symmetric under a top-to-bottom flip with hot and cold swapped (and under a left-to-right flip). So a developed flow and its mirror image must stay mirror images through every step. Running each stage on both and comparing pinpoints any stage that breaks the symmetry.

It found the coarse-plate bug: a 1.9% mirror error per step in the pressure solve, now 0.0003% (rounding). It passes across grid sizes, plate widths and temperatures, the free stream, both brush tools, both pressure solvers, both advection schemes and both edge modes. The one asymmetry left is the checkerboard order in diffusion, about 0.01% per step, far below anything visible.

10What changed along the way

From class sketch to GPU

  • CPU original: 72 × 48 grid, 7 pressure passes, 2 diffusion passes, an optional one-time ripple to break symmetry.
  • GPU port: same method, 16× the cells.
  • Realism work: solid plates in the pressure solve, multigrid, MacCormack advection, plate flickers.
  • Features: flow tracers, plate labels and color bar, the brush, the finer temperature grid, custom colors, responsive layout.

Ideas tried and dropped

  • Reusing last step's pressure as a starting guess made the edges worse.
  • A zoomed color range was replaced by choosing the colors directly.
  • Relief shading, a stir brush and the "one fluid" caption were removed to keep the page focused.

A lesson from the finer temperature grid. In the project this idea came from, the fine field was passive dye, which just gets carried along. Here temperature drives the flow, and detail much finer than the velocity grid can't push the fluid itself; it gets wound up passively into a marbled look. Turning heat diffusion down too far makes that worse, so the default (0.0003) was chosen by eye as a balance between sharp plumes and that marbling.

11Limits

  • 2D. Real convection is 3D; 2D flows hold on to large swirls longer.
  • No turbulence model. At Ra ≈ 10⁷ the thinnest layers are about one cell thick, so the smallest details depend on grid size.
  • Boussinesq. Valid for modest temperature differences, where density changes only matter for buoyancy.
  • Chaotic. Two runs never match in detail, so compare averages over time or over runs, not single frames.
  • Not a validated engineering solver. It is checked for symmetry, conservation, textbook cases and convergence, not against laboratory data.
